01 About

From server racks to research papers

I'm a Computer Science PhD student at The University of Alabama, advised by Dr. Shahram Rahimi. I work on two sides of the same problem: securing the pipelines that build and ship machine-learning models, and putting AI agents to work detecting and responding to attacks on real enterprise networks.

I didn't set out to study computers. I began in Mechanical Engineering, took a single programming course, enjoyed it so much that I switched majors, and haven't looked back.

Before research, I spent six years as a systems administrator at Mississippi State University. I ran servers, virtual desktops and thousands of user accounts, and I patched systems against live vulnerability reports. That experience shapes how I do research: a defense only counts if it holds up under real deployment constraints and the people who have to maintain it. Today I bring the same mindset to my role as a Systems Analyst in UA's Department of Computer Science.

I also enjoy the people side of technology. I've trained student workers, managed a department's social media, and helped plan departmental events.

02 Research

Securing AI, and AI for security

My work sits where machine learning meets operational security: how ML systems get attacked across their lifecycle, and how autonomous agents can defend networks without taking humans out of the loop.

Featured · First author

Towards Secure MLOps: Surveying Attacks, Mitigation Strategies, and Research Challenges

ACM Transactions on AI Security and Privacy · 2026

A 35-page survey that applies MITRE ATLAS, together with MITRE ATT&CK and white and grey literature, to map attacks across the MLOps and LLMOps lifecycle. It introduces a three-family taxonomy, a threat model that runs from black-box outsiders to privileged insiders, and a matching taxonomy of mitigations, each grounded in real incidents and red-team exercises.

The editors identified it as the journal's first accepted manuscript.

03 Projects

Featured projects

First author · arXiv 2026

Agentra: A Supervisable Multi-Agent Framework for Enterprise Intrusion Response

The problem

Security teams receive more IDS, EDR and XDR alerts than analysts can triage, and static playbooks can't adapt. Handing response to unsupervised LLM agents trades one risk for another: an agent that overreacts can disrupt the business it is meant to protect.

What we built

A multi-agent intrusion response system in which role-scoped agents turn alerts into response plans mapped to MITRE ATT&CK, MITRE D3FEND and NIST CSF 2.0. A bounded Planner–Validator loop reviews each plan, a security gateway mediates threat-intelligence lookups, actions come from a risk-scored catalog, and every step lands in an append-only audit trail.

What it showed

On 120 security events, the strongest configuration raised false-positive-aware response F1 from 0.61 (a static CACAO v2.0 playbook) to 0.84 and kept the harmful-action rate at the baseline's 0.0%. Planner-only setups overreacted. Analysts stay in the approval loop throughout.

First author · IEEE CogMI 2026

The Value of Spike Timing: A Leakage-Resistant Benchmark of SNN Design Choices for Network Intrusion Detection

The problem

Spiking neural networks are a promising, energy-efficient option for intrusion detection, but it isn't clear which design choices actually matter. Results can also look better than they are when training and test data aren't cleanly separated.

What we built

A benchmark of 27 SNN configurations, nine neuron models crossed with three spike encodings, evaluated on four network intrusion detection datasets under leakage-resistant protocols.

What it showed

Spike timing matters. Latency coding, which maps a feature's magnitude to when a spike fires, reliably improves macro-F1. Sparse inputs don't guarantee sparse activity inside the network. A journal extension is in progress with Dr. Sayanton Dibbo.

Read the paper

Co-author · arXiv 2026

AgenticCyOps: Securing Multi-Agentic AI Integration in Enterprise Cyber Operations

The problem

LLM-powered multi-agent systems introduce risks that deterministic pipelines don't have, yet most security research looks only at narrow, prompt-level attacks.

What we built

A breakdown of multi-agent attack surfaces across the component, coordination and protocol layers, plus five defensive principles mapped to NIST, ISO 27001, GDPR and the EU AI Act. We applied them to a Security Operations Center workflow built on the Model Context Protocol.

What it showed

Most documented attacks trace back to tool orchestration and memory management. The design blocks three of four representative attack chains within their first two steps and cuts exploitable trust boundaries by at least 72% compared with a flat multi-agent system.

Read the paper

Co-author · arXiv 2026

Where Rules End and Judges Begin: Measuring the Judgment Boundary in Multi-Agent Systems Security

The problem

LLM-based multi-agent systems use tools, share memory and delegate tasks while handling adversarial content, but their defenses are usually tested against one attack type at a time.

What we built

A defense organized around five principles: a cascade of 28 deterministic checks handles clear-cut cases, and a panel of four LLM judges resolves the rest.

What it showed

Across four domains, attack success fell from about 30% to about 3%, and the deterministic checks caught 78% of blocked attacks. In security operations, only about a quarter of proposals needed the judges. Rules covered clear policy violations, and judges covered attempts to misrepresent intent. The paper also reports where both approaches fall short.

01

Foundations, then freedom

Give students a solid foundation, make it enjoyable, then leave room to explore. One course taught that way turned me from a mechanical engineering major into a computer scientist.

02

Real systems, real stakes

Concepts stick when students see where they're actually used. I tie every topic to a documented incident, real data, or a production system I ran as an administrator.

03

Close the feedback loop

Sharp follow-up questions show me that learning is happening. Minute papers and anonymous mid-semester feedback tell me what to keep and what to change.
